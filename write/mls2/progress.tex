\section{Key research achieved (ODSA)}

ODSA operates on the along-track picks published with Bedmap1 to 3 rather than a
gridded product (Ch.~\ref{review}). The tracks used are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:Bedmap+Ockenden_map}. The ODSA pipeline splits tracks into segments, cuts them into $50~\mathrm{km}$ windows (when possible) with a $25~\mathrm{km}$ step, applies a Hann taper, and fits the spectrum over $250~\mathrm{m}$ to $50~\mathrm{km}$ using a Lomb--Scargle periodogram. 
Fig.~\ref{fig:catalogue_examples} shows a subset of ODSA's catalogue, catalogue entries are paired with an analysed window in five of the seven regions in(Fig.~\ref{fig:7regions}) as proof of concept. I analysed a total of 651 windows, 650 of which fall in a classified (non-dune) cell. My main results include: a transect bounds the landscape class but does not identify it with only RES data. Of the 11 catalogue entries, the median number still admissible per window is 2. The windows split into 402 degenerate, 135 resolved, 84 out-of-catalogue and 30 resolved-with-external.

In the case of my catalogue I found that a combination of classifiers narrowed down the set of admissible landscape classes more than any single classifier for the windows analysed (Fig.~\ref{fig:hypothesis_test}). The figure shows the best combination at each number of classifiers. Resolved fraction peaks with $\beta$, relief and velocity (27\%). Adding elevation lowers it to 25\% and nearly doubles the out-of-catalogue rate, from 7\% to 13\%. However, elevation is the classifier that separates the most classes of the Ockenden et al.~\cite{Ockenden_2026} classification (Fig.~\ref{fig:ockenden_matrix}). Their classification is constructed from gridded bed elevation, so this is expected. Yet I found that no combination of classifiers separates their alpine and selective-erosion classes from one another.

The classes reported by Ockenden et al.~\cite{Ockenden_2026} are partly defined by roughness metrics (RMS slope, hill counts, Fourier fractal dimension), yet over all valid 650 windows my roughness measures ($\beta$, relief, and the descriptors) barely separate them. 
A window can straddle two cells of different class. When I restricted to the 421 windows whose second-nearest cell carries the same class as the nearest, the seven descriptors together separate three of the six class pairs, including Alpine (subglacial) from Selective erosion (relict). The differing outcomes for roughness may be due to their classes resulting from an inverted, interpolated 2D grid (space-domain), while mine result from spectral measurements from 1D picks along track. The two roughness descriptions of the same ground therefore disagree, which is consistent with a gridded product not carrying the texture that a transect measures.

\section*{Data Management}

Data will be published adhering to FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), ensuring transparency and accessibility. The final bed topography datasets will be published at the Australian Antarctic Data Centre (ADC) under an open licence. All production model outputs will be published with unique DOIs at repositories aligned with the corresponding papers. Model outputs will be archived to tape at National Computational Infrastructure (NCI) using existing Securing Antarctica's Environmental Future (SAEF) resources, as well as backed up to storage available through Monash MASSIVE M3 account aligned with project supervisor Dr McCormack. All papers published through this project will be open access, and tier 1 journals will be targeted.
