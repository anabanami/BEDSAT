\section{Key research achieved (ODSA)}

ODSA operates on the along-track picks published with Bedmap1 to 3 instead of a gridded product, which allows us to measure the survey data directly and avoid the interpolation issues described in Ch.~\ref{review}. The tracks used are visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:Bedmap+Ockenden_map}. 
The ODSA pipeline splits tracks into segments, cuts them into $50~\mathrm{km}$ windows (when possible) with a $25~\mathrm{km}$ step, applies a Hann taper, and fits the spectrum over $250~\mathrm{m}$ to $50~\mathrm{km}$ using a Lomb--Scargle periodogram. The morphometric statistics measured from
the RES data form an 11-element landscape vector (Tables~\ref{tab:axes} and~\ref{tab:descriptors}). Every vector classifier has a $\pm 2\sigma$ uncertainty envelope, so no axis is assumed to be exact. The vector is compared to a literature-based catalogue of 11 landscape archetypes. When a window matches more than one archetype, the pipeline names the axis responsible and the observable needed to break the tie (Table~\ref{tab:externals}).

Fig.~\ref{fig:catalogue_examples} shows ODSA's catalogue applied to the seven regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:7regions}), with each catalogue entry paired with an analysed window. We analysed a total of 651 windows, 650 of which fall in a classified (non-dune) cell. Our main results include: a transect bounds the landscape class but does not identify it with only RES data. Of the 11 catalogue entries, the median number still admissible per window is 2. The windows split into 402 degenerate, 135 resolved, 84 out-of-catalogue and 30 resolved-with-external.
In the case of our catalogue we found that a combination of our classifiers narrowed down the set of admissible landscape classes more than any single classifier for the windows analysed (Fig.~\ref{fig:hypothesis_test}).  The figure shows the best combination at each number of classifiers. Resolution peaks with $\beta$, relief and velocity (27\%). Adding elevation lowers it to 25\% and nearly doubles the out-of-catalogue rate, from 7\% to 13\%, so elevation converts windows rather than resolving them. However, elevation is the classifier that separates the most classes of the Ockenden et al.~\cite{Ockenden_2026} classification (Fig.~\ref{fig:ockenden_matrix}). Their classification is constructed from gridded bed elevation, so this is expected. Yet we found that no combination of our classifiers separates their alpine and selective-erosion classes from one another.

The classes reported by Ockenden et al.~\cite{Ockenden_2026} are partly defined by roughness metrics (rms slope, hill counts, Fourier fractal dimension), yet over all valid 650 windows our roughness measures ($\beta$, relief, and the descriptors) separate them barely at all. 
One of our windows can straddle two different class cells. When we restricted to the 421 windows whose second-nearest cell carries the same class as the nearest, the seven descriptors together separate three of the six class pairs, including Alpine (subglacial) from Selective erosion (relict). The differing outcomes for roughness may be due to their classes resulting from an inverted, interpolated 2D grid (space-domain), while ours result from spectral measurements from 1D picks along track. The two roughness descriptions of the same ground therefore disagree, which is consistent with a gridded product not carrying the texture that a transect measures.

\section*{Data Management}

Data will be published adhering to FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), ensuring transparency and accessibility. The final bed topography datasets will be published at the Australian Antarctic Data Centre (AADC) under an open licence. All production model outputs will be published with unique DOIs at repositories aligned with the corresponding papers. Model outputs will be archived to tape at NCI using existing SAEF resources, as well as backed up to storage available through Monash MASSIVE M3 account aligned with project supervisor Dr McCormack. All papers published through this project will be open access, and tier 1 journals will be targeted.
